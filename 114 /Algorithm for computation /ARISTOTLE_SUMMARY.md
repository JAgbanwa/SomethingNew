# Summary of changes for run 20aaea3e-c1b6-480d-ad72-9f279e243f6e
I designed, implemented, benchmarked and (where it is mathematical) machine-verified a suite of grid algorithms for finding rational `d` with integer solutions of `36n³ − 19 = −2d·x²·(−(x+6n) + √((x+6n)² + (36n³−19)/x))`, targeted at the family `n = 3m`, `x = 12u+7` and packaged as 1–2 hour Charity Engine work units with the digit-band escalation you asked for.

**The key idea, and why it really is a search "for d".** `d` is determined by `(n,x)`, so the only way to search over `d` is to search over the integer `U = 2dx²` (the numerator of `d`). I proved in Lean (`RequestProject/Algorithm.lean`, no `sorry`, builds cleanly) that this turns the problem into a program: `DEquation.sat_iff_dSweep` says `(n,x)` solves the equation for `d` **iff** `U = 2dx²` satisfies `U² = c·x` (so `x` runs only over the divisors of `U²`), `n` is a root of the cubic `36n³ − 12Un = c + 2Ux + 19`, and the branch condition holds — so one enumerates `d` and *solves* for `n` by a cube root instead of brute-forcing `(m,u)`. Three further proved facts drive the design: `abs_n_le_abs_U` (`|n| ≤ |U|`, which calibrates the digit bands exactly), `sat_U_mod_twelve` (`x ≡ 7 mod 12` forces `U ≡ 5, 11 mod 12`, a free factor 6; the extra condition `n = 3m` costs and gains nothing), and `mordell_of_sat`/`sat_of_mordell` (for fixed `x` the solutions are the integral points of the Mordell curve `V² = Z³ − 432x³(x³+57)`, the one view with no bound on `n`).

**Programs** (`grid/`, plain C and Python, no dependencies): `dsweep.c` — the exhaustive d-first sweep (measured 2.4·10⁹ certificates per core-hour at `|U| ~ 10⁹`, 1.6·10⁹ at `10¹⁴`); `dsmooth.c` — the same sweep restricted to `B`-smooth certificates, which can be enumerated for 20- and 30-digit `U` and is the sweep that can reach the high bands (three of the eleven known certificates are smooth); `xscan.c` — the per-`x` scan, complete in `m`, at 5.4·10¹¹ values of `m` per core-hour and per `x`; `xcurve.gp`/`xcurve.py` — the Mordell–Weil phase, whose group law amplifies a generator to 20-digit heights. Plus `verify.py` (exact verifier that turns a hit into a certified `d`), `make_jobs.py` (work-unit manifests sized from the measured rates) and `run_task.sh` (node wrapper). All are validated: `dsweep --general` and `dsmooth --test` independently re-discover the same known certificates, `xscan --selftest` checks that no perfect square is ever rejected, and `xcurve.py --selftest` verifies the curve correspondence on all eleven known solutions.

**`ALGORITHMS.md`** gives the full derivation, the costs, the work-unit table for each digit band (band 10 ≈ 200 sweep units; band 13 ≈ 2·10⁵; beyond band 15 the exhaustive sweeps stop and the smooth-certificate and curve units take over) and an honest yield analysis, including that the known solutions appear at roughly one per decade of `|n|` but that none of the eleven lies in the requested family (their `x` are `≡ 0, 3, 4 mod 12`), although no local obstruction to that family exists.

**Computation run here** (recorded in `search/run8/`): with `x ≡ 7 mod 12`, the exhaustive sweep covered every certificate `|U| < 2·10¹⁰` and the smooth sweep every 50-smooth certificate with `10¹⁰ ≤ |U| < 10¹⁸` — about 5·10¹¹ `(U,x)` pairs — with no solution found; by `|n| ≤ |U|` this settles the family for certificates of ten digits or fewer. `README.md` and `SEARCH_NOTES.md` were updated, and the Properties table now lists the five new proved results.

# Summary of changes for run 94b5cf72-01f3-422f-bb3c-aca64a47b623
**Answer.** Reading the square root as the principal (nonnegative) real root, the equation

`36n³ − 19 = −2d·x²·(−(x+6n) + √((x+6n)² + (36n³−19)/x))`

is *equivalent* (theorem `DEquation.sat_iff` in `RequestProject/Main.lean`) to two purely rational conditions:

* `36n³ − 19 = 4d²x³ + 4dx³ + 24dnx²` (the equation with the radical cleared), and
* `2dx + x + 6n ≤ 0` (which branch of the root is taken).

From this I derived a complete criterion (`DEquation.exists_d_iff`): integers `n`, `x ≠ 0` admit some rational `d` **iff** `x²(x+6n)² + x(36n³−19)` is a perfect square, and then `d` is uniquely determined by `(n,x)` (`DEquation.sat_unique`), namely `d = (e − (x+6n))/(2x)` with `e = −√(x²(x+6n)² + x(36n³−19))/|x|`. For fixed `d` the first condition is a cubic Thue-type equation `36n³ − 24dnx² − 4d(d+1)x³ = 19`, so each admissible `d` has only finitely many integer solutions.

Using that criterion I ran searches (exhaustive over `|n|,|x| ≤ 10⁶`; over `|n| ≤ 2·10⁵, |x| ≤ 4·10⁶`; and a search by the parameter `t = 2dx²` with `|n| ≤ 7·10⁵, |t| ≤ 2·10⁵`, which reaches arbitrarily large `|x|`). They produced exactly six rational values of `d`:
